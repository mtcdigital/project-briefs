Meme Coin Trading Bot

A bot that reads crypto Twitter and is supposed to buy meme coins on its own. The machine works; the strategy is the part we can't prove yet.

Blocked Updated Sep 1, 2026 7 min read

⚡ The 10-second version

  1. I built a bot that reads crypto Twitter and is supposed to buy meme coins on its own, then sell them at a profit.
  2. The bot works. The strategy doesn't — and today I found out the numbers I'd been using to prove it worked were measuring the wrong thing entirely.
  3. There's $130 of real money in the wallet. It has never made a single trade, and the bot is switched off right now.

🧭 What this actually is

A program that watches 95 hand-picked crypto Twitter accounts. When several of them mention the same new coin at once, it's supposed to buy that coin automatically, then sell it as the price rises — or cut the loss if it falls.

The idea: if the people who are good at spotting coins all point at the same one, that's worth a small bet.

🔎 What I've learned so far

Today was mostly finding out that things I believed were wrong. In order of how much they cost us:

1. I was measuring the wrong thing all along.
Every "does this work?" number compared a tweet from April to what the coin is worth today — five months later. But the bot sells after four hours. That's like judging a day trader by looking at where stocks were five years later. The typical big winner in our data took 323 days to get there. The bot would have sold every one of them the same afternoon.
2. 96% of our data was fake.
The database has 38,000 tweets. It turns out 36,500 of them were generated by a built-in testing tool that had been left switched on, and they were saved into the same place as the real ones with nothing marking them as fake. Only about 1,600 tweets were real. Two earlier conclusions were built on the fake ones and had to be thrown out.
3. The bot had no way to sell a losing coin.
The original code only sold when a coin went up — at 2x, 5x and 10x. There was no rule for when it went down. Since 72% of these coins go to zero, it would have held every loser all the way down. Now fixed and tested.
4. It once bought a coin it never actually owned.
Back in April it sent a buy order, got a "message received" reply, and wrote down "I own this now" — without checking the purchase actually went through. It didn't. The bot then spent 14 hours trying to sell 72 billion coins that never existed, failing 126 times. Now fixed: it checks the wallet before believing anything.
5. The one genuinely useful discovery.
We split every coin into two piles — ones that were brand new when they got tweeted, versus ones that already had a real market. The difference was enormous. See the numbers section.

The finding that matters

Same tweets, same accounts. The only difference is whether the coin already had a market before someone tweeted about it.

Brand-new coins — dead now94%248 coins. This is what the bot chases today.
Established coins — dead now3%74 coins. 97% still alive.
New coins that got big1.2%Reached $1M value
Established coins that got big20.3%Reached $1M value
A coin that already had a market was about 17 times more likely to become a big winner. That single measurement is worth more than the rest of the bot put together.

The honest counterweight

  • 94% of everyone trading Solana meme coins loses money. That's from a study of 304,000 real wallets over 90 days. Only 6% made a profit, and of those, 88% made under $100.
  • 89% of individual bets lose even in our best-case setup. The typical bet loses about 40%.
  • The gains all come from a tiny handful of enormous winners. That's a lottery shape — lose small often, occasionally win big. It is not a paycheck.

🔀 The fork in the road

Everett has to pick one of three. He has $100–130 to risk and doesn't want this eating his time.

Option A — Stop here

Completely defensible

  • Costs nothing, keeps the $130
  • Zero further time
  • We never find out
  • The 17x finding goes unused

Option B — Change what it buys

What I'm recommending

  • Buy coins that already have a market, not brand-new ones
  • Hold for months instead of 4 hours
  • Costs about $0/month to run — no fast scraping needed
  • ~2 hours to switch over, then it runs itself
  • Still a lottery ticket, just a better priced one
  • Barely a "trading bot" any more — more an automatic small-bet picker

Option C — Prove the fast version properly

Only if genuinely curious

  • Would give a real answer instead of a guess
  • ~$5/day of data costs for two weeks
  • Two weeks of waiting before you learn anything
  • Might just confirm it doesn't work
Worth knowing: Everett already runs a second system — a daily email that watches 110 crypto accounts and reports what they agree on. That is basically Option B already, minus the automatic buying. The meme coin bot has been the experiment; the daily email has quietly been the useful thing.

📖 Words you might not know

Meme coin
A crypto coin made as a joke or a trend, with no product behind it. Prices swing enormously. Most go to zero within days.
Solana
A blockchain — think of it as the network the coins live on. Cheap and fast, which is why most meme coins are made there.
Wallet
Where the crypto is kept. Ours holds about $130. It has an address anyone can look up, and a secret key that controls it.
Stop loss
An automatic "sell if it drops this far" rule, so one bad coin can't wipe you out. Ours sells half at −30% and the rest at −40%.
Market cap
Roughly what the whole coin is worth. A brand-new meme coin starts around $4,000. Reaching $1,000,000 counts as a real success.
Rug / rug pull
When the person who made the coin takes all the money and disappears. Extremely common — it's most of that 94% dying.
Scraping
Automatically reading a website (here, Twitter) to collect information. Costs money if you use a paid service to do it.
Paper trading
Running the bot with pretend money to see what it would have done. No real funds at risk.
Backtest
Replaying old data through the strategy to check if it would have made money. This is the thing we got wrong today.
